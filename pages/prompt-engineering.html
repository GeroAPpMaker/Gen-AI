What is Prompt Engineering?

ðŸ“–
Prompt Engineering is the art and science of crafting instructions (prompts) that guide an AI to produce the most accurate, useful, and relevant output. Think of it as learning to speak AI's language.

The 5 Principles of Professional Prompting

1

Be Specific

Vague input = vague output

Include grade level, subject, duration, number of students, and specific requirements. The more detail you give, the better the output.

âŒ "Make a quiz" âœ… "Create a 10-question multiple choice quiz on fractions for 4th graders with an answer key"
2

AI doesn't know your classroom

Provide background about your students, school setting, constraints, and goals. Context transforms generic outputs into personalized ones.

âŒ "Write a parent email" âœ… "Write a parent email about our upcoming field trip to the science museum on March 15th. Include permission slip deadline (March 10), cost (\$12), lunch details (bag lunch needed), and bus departure time (8:30 AM)"
3

Tell AI who to be

Starting with "Act as..." or "You are a..." dramatically changes the quality and tone of responses. The AI adjusts its expertise, vocabulary, and approach.

âŒ "Explain photosynthesis" âœ… "You are a fun, engaging 3rd grade science teacher. Explain photosynthesis using simple analogies a 9-year-old would understand. Use a cooking metaphor."
4

Tell AI HOW you want the output

âŒ "Give me vocabulary words" âœ… "Create a vocabulary list in a table format with 3 columns: Word, Definition (student-friendly), and Example Sentence. Include 10 words related to ecosystems for 6th graders."

Professional prompt engineers rarely get perfect results on the first try. Use follow-up prompts to refine, adjust, and improve the output.

Professional techniques that take your prompts to the next level

Technique 1

ðŸ”— Chain-of-Thought Prompting

What: Ask AI to think step-by-step before giving a final answer. This dramatically improves reasoning quality.

âŒ Without Chain-of-Thought
"Create a rubric for a persuasive essay."

Generic rubric, may miss important criteria

âœ… With Chain-of-Thought
"I need a rubric for a 7th grade persuasive essay. Before creating it, think through: 1) What skills should a persuasive essay demonstrate? 2) What does each skill look like at different performance levels? 3) How should the criteria be weighted? Then create the rubric based on your analysis."

Thoughtful, well-structured rubric with justified criteria

ðŸ”‘ Trigger Phrases:
"Think step by step..." "Before answering, consider..." "Walk me through your reasoning..." "First analyze, then create..." "Let's break this down..."
Technique 2

ðŸ“ Few-Shot Learning (Show Examples)

What: Provide 1-3 examples of what you want. AI learns the pattern and produces similar quality output.

Prompt with Examples:

"Write discussion questions for a novel study. Match this style:

Example 1:

"How does the author use the setting of the dusty farm to reflect Charlotte's loneliness? Find a specific passage that shows this."

Example 2:

"Wilbur says 'I don't want to die.' How does this moment change the way we see him as a character? Connect this to a time you felt afraid."

Now create 5 more questions like these for chapters 10-15."

Why this works:
  • AI matches your style, depth, and complexity level
  • Questions require higher-order thinking (not just recall)
  • Include text evidence requirements
  • Personal connection component
Technique 3

ðŸš« Constraint Prompting

What: Tell AI what NOT to do. Constraints often produce better results than open-ended instructions.

Content Constraints
  • "Do NOT include any content that requires internet access"
  • "Avoid using technical jargon â€” all terms must be defined"
  • "Do not reference events after 2020 (historical accuracy)"
Format Constraints
  • "Keep each explanation under 2 sentences"
  • "Do NOT use passive voice"
  • "No more than 5 bullet points per section"
Pedagogical Constraints
  • "Do NOT give the answer directly â€” use guiding questions instead"
  • "Avoid worksheet-style activities â€” focus on hands-on learning"
  • "Do not assume students have prior knowledge of fractions"
